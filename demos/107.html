Matrix multiplication with exponent at most 9/4 (conceptual, result 107)

Improves the arithmetic complexity of matrix multiplication: over $\mathbb C$, square $n\times n$ multiplication costs $O_\varepsilon(n^{9/4+\varepsilon})$ operations, and rectangular products with sufficiently large inner dimension can be computed in $n^{2+o(1)}$. Over every field, including finite fields and positive characteristic, $\omega<2.371054886006746$.

Context

The exponent $\omega(F)$ is the infimum of $\tau$ such that two $n\times n$ matrices over $F$ can be multiplied with $O(n^\tau)$ arithmetic operations. This result proves $\omega(\mathbb C)\le 9/4$, with the stronger dual exponent $\alpha>0.465$: an inner dimension $n^a$ with $a>0.465$ permits rectangular multiplication of cost $n^{2+o(1)}$ in characteristic zero. The rectangular exponent at aspect ratio $0.709$ satisfies $\omega(\mathbb C;1,0.709,1)<2.092$. The arithmetic model counts additions, subtractions, and multiplications in finite division-free programs, with arbitrary positive exponent slack. Outside finitely many positive characteristics, $\omega<2.258$; unconditionally, $\omega(F)<2.371054886006746$ for every field $F$. The square bound implies the earlier $2.258$ headline bound. Formalized statements cover both the complex square/dual/rectangular bounds and the every-field bound.

Relevance

Sharpens the central bilinear-complexity problem of matrix multiplication and separates the cost of square products from rectangular products with large inner dimensions. The characteristic-independent bound shows that fast multiplication is not special to complex or zero-characteristic arithmetic: the same asymptotic improvement applies over finite fields and all fixed fields. The dual-exponent threshold identifies when matrix multiplication can be performed essentially at the quadratic lower bound, with consequences for algorithms that reduce to rectangular products, such as parsing, dynamic programming, and algebraic path problems. The formalization removes numerical inequalities from the hypotheses, making the bounds unconditional in the arithmetic model.
